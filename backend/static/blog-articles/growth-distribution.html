Growth and Distribution for Software Products

Turn useful content, community participation, paid search, and honest competitor comparisons into a measurable acquisition plan.

Growth · Practical guide

By SeeYourSEO

Publishing a site is only the start. Growth work helps the right people discover a product, understand its value, and take a meaningful next step. Search optimization, content marketing, community promotion, paid advertising, and product operations overlap, but each channel needs its own goal and measurement.

Keep the terms clear: organic SEO earns unpaid visibility in search results. Paid search buys ad placements through an auction. Content marketing and community promotion distribute helpful material through channels where an audience already spends time.

Step 1: Pick one audience and one outcome

Before writing or buying traffic, decide who the campaign serves and what you want that person to do. Choose an outcome such as a qualified signup, demo request, purchase, or completed product action. “More visits” alone is not a useful success measure.

  • Write one sentence describing the audience, the problem, and the product's distinct benefit.
  • Choose a landing page that explains the offer and makes the next action obvious.
  • Set a time window and a spending limit for experiments before launch.
  • Check that signup or purchase events are measured before spending on ads.

For a new product, start with a small experiment that can teach you something. Do not scale a channel because it produces cheap clicks if those visitors do not become qualified users.

Step 2: Write useful articles that can earn attention

“Soft articles” work best when they solve a real reader problem and disclose the author's relationship to the product. Build a small editorial plan from customer questions, support requests, community discussions, and search intent.

  • How-to article: show a process from start to finish, including examples and common failure points.
  • Comparison: explain who each option suits, what you compared, and where your product has limits.
  • Case study: document a real project, method, constraints, and measured outcome. Label estimates and avoid inventing results.
  • Template or tool: give readers something useful they can apply without buying your product.

Use an accurate headline, link to evidence, and include a relevant product call to action only where it helps the reader. If an article is sponsored or contains a material commercial relationship, identify that clearly near the article or recommendation. The FTC's native advertising guidance explains why ad content should not be disguised as independent editorial.

A useful starting point is the SEO research and launch workflow, which shows how to turn audience questions into topics and pages.

Step 3: Promote where the right community already is

Choose a few channels that match the product instead of posting everywhere. Prepare a short demo, a clear description, and an honest explanation of what you learned or built. Read the current rules for each community before posting.

  • Product Hunt can support a coordinated product launch. Prepare the listing and launch-day responses in advance.
  • Hacker News has Show HN for projects that are relevant to its technical audience. Explain what is new and be ready to answer questions.
  • Indie Hackers is suited to transparent builder lessons, progress updates, and practical feedback requests.
  • BetaList and focused software directories may help early adopters find a new product.
  • DEV Community, Hashnode, or Medium can host an original article for their readers. Follow each platform's rules on republishing and canonical links.

Make each post useful on its own. Do not send unsolicited bulk messages, repeat the same promotion across unrelated communities, or ask people to post reviews they do not believe. Track visits with campaign tags (UTMs) so you can tell which source led to engaged users.

Before spending: check LTV, CAC, and payback

A paid campaign is sustainable only when the value of the customers it acquires can cover what it costs to acquire and serve them. Compare customer lifetime value (LTV) with customer acquisition cost (CAC), and check how long it takes to earn CAC back.

Core formulas
Contribution LTV = average revenue per customer over the paid lifetime × gross margin
CAC = acquisition costs ÷ new paying customers acquired
LTV:CAC = contribution LTV ÷ CAC
CAC payback months = CAC ÷ monthly gross profit per customer

For channel decisions, calculate a channel CAC using the costs and new paying customers attributable to that channel. Include ad spend, creative production, agency or contractor fees, and other campaign costs. Also track a fully loaded blended CAC that includes your broader sales and marketing costs. Keep the time period and customer definition consistent.

Example: if a subscription earns $40 per month at an 80% gross margin and a customer stays for an average of 20 paid months, estimated contribution LTV is $640. If a paid campaign costs $4,000 all-in and brings 10 new paying customers, CAC is $400, LTV:CAC is 1.6, and CAC payback is 12.5 months ($400 ÷ $32 monthly gross profit). This is a calculation example, not a target or forecast.

  • LTV greater than CAC is a necessary check, but it does not by itself prove the business is profitable. Gross profit must also cover overhead, support not included in the margin, taxes, and the return you need.
  • Use actual retention and gross margin by customer cohort when available. Early LTV estimates are uncertain, so use conservative assumptions and update them as customers mature.
  • Payback matters for cash flow: a campaign can look profitable over a long lifetime while taking too long to recover its cost.
  • There is no universal LTV:CAC target for every business. Compare channels, customer segments, margins, and cash constraints that fit your model.

For additional definitions and calculation details, see Stripe's SaaS CAC guide and ChartMogul's CAC payback explanation.

Step 4: Test paid search without confusing it with SEO

In paid search, you bid on search terms that may trigger an ad; you are buying ad visibility, not owning the keyword. Google Ads offers keyword matching options that determine how closely a search must relate to a selected keyword. Matching behavior can include related meanings, so review the actual search terms that triggered ads.

  1. Separate intent: create distinct groups for people seeking a solution, comparing products, or searching for your brand.
  2. Match the landing page: the ad promise, selected query, and page content should agree.
  3. Start with a controlled budget: use a narrow geography and a short test window where appropriate.
  4. Review search terms: exclude irrelevant queries with negative keywords. Google explains how negative keywords work and where their match behavior differs.
  5. Measure conversions: count qualified actions, not only impressions and clicks. Google Ads provides conversion tracking for actions such as signups and purchases.

Use Keyword Planner to explore demand, then check the live search results and your own campaign data. A large estimated search volume does not guarantee that a query will convert for your product.

Step 5: Reach people comparing your product with alternatives

People searching for a competitor, a competitor alternative, or a product comparison often have a specific need. You can serve that need with a genuinely useful comparison page, integration guide, migration guide, or listing in a relevant directory.

  • Compare products using clear criteria such as use case, features, pricing model, integrations, and limitations.
  • Use current, verifiable facts. Link to source pages and add a “last checked” date when details can change.
  • Explain which product fits which user; do not claim to be independent if you sell one of the compared products.
  • Make a separate landing page for a distinct alternative or migration task only when it provides real value, not just a swapped competitor name.
  • Submit to directories such as AlternativeTo, SaaSHub, or OpenAlternative only when the product fits their audience and submission rules.

You can also test ads for competitor-related searches, but check the ad platform policy and the laws in the markets you target. Google Ads says trademark use as a keyword is treated differently from using a trademark in ad text; confusing or misleading ads may be restricted. Read the current Google Ads trademark policy before writing ads. Never imitate a competitor's brand or imply an affiliation that does not exist.

Step 6: Build durable distribution, not just launch-day attention

A launch can create a short burst of visits. A durable acquisition system comes from useful pages, repeatable outreach, customer referrals, integrations, and a product people recommend. Choose one or two channels that produced qualified interest and improve them before adding more.

  • Turn common questions from sales or support into articles, product documentation, or comparison pages.
  • Ask partners, customers, and maintainers about relevant integrations or co-created resources; make the value clear to both audiences.
  • Keep your listings accurate when features, pricing, or availability change.
  • Use backlinks as a result of useful resources and real editorial relationships, not as a bulk metric to manufacture.

Step 7: Read the funnel and choose the next experiment

Use Google Search Console for organic search queries, impressions, clicks, and indexing. Use Google Analytics or an equivalent analytics tool to understand sessions and on-site actions. Use your ad platform's conversion reports for paid campaigns. Keep the channels separate in reports so you can compare them fairly.

ChannelUseful signalsQuestion to ask
Organic searchQueries, impressions, clicks, landing pagesAre the right topics finding the right pages?
Articles and communitiesTagged visits, engaged sessions, qualified signupsDid the post help the intended audience take a next step?
Paid searchSpend, search terms, conversions, cost per qualified actionDoes the campaign acquire users at an acceptable cost?
Competitor comparisonsRelevant impressions, referral visits, assisted conversionsDoes the comparison answer a real evaluation question?

For each experiment, write down the hypothesis, audience, landing page, budget or effort, date range, and result. Change one major factor at a time when practical. Keep tests that bring qualified users; revise or stop those that only create activity.

A simple four-week operating plan

  • Week 1 — Learn: collect audience questions and pick one conversion goal.
  • Week 2 — Publish: create one useful article or comparison page with evidence and a clear next step.
  • Week 3 — Distribute: share it in one or two relevant communities and test a small paid search campaign only if conversion tracking works.
  • Week 4 — Review: compare qualified actions by channel, record what you learned, and decide what to improve or test next.

For keyword and audience research, see the keyword research guide. For page structure and useful content, see the on-page SEO checklist.

Good growth compounds: make a useful promise, put it in front of the right people, measure whether it helped, and keep improving the channel that earns trust.
